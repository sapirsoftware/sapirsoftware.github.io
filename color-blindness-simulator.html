The types, and how common they are

TypeWhat's affectedRoughly how common
Deuteranomaly / deuteranopiaGreen cones weak or missing. Reds, greens and browns get confused.About 6% of men (mostly the mild form)
Protanomaly / protanopiaRed cones weak or missing. Reds also look dark, so red text on black nearly disappears.About 2% of men
Tritanomaly / tritanopiaBlue cones weak or missing. Blue and green, and yellow and pink, get confused.Under 0.01%, equal in men and women
AchromatopsiaNo working cones: only light and dark.About 1 in 30,000

Figures are for people of Northern European descent; overall, about 1 in 12 men and 1 in 200 women have some red-green deficiency. Use the severity slider for the milder, much more common forms.

What to fix when something disappears

  • Never use color alone. Red and green status dots, chart lines told apart only by hue, and form errors shown only as a red border all fail WCAG 1.4.1. Add a label, an icon, a pattern or a direct annotation.
  • Lean on lightness. Two colors with different luminance stay distinct for every viewer. Check pairs with the contrast checker; here's what the ratios mean.
  • Pick palettes built for it. Blue and orange work for nearly everyone. Viridis and Okabe-Ito are well-tested palettes for charts.

The simulation uses the model of Machado, Oliveira and Fernandes (2009), applied in linear RGB; lower severities blend toward normal vision. It models an average viewer, not anyone in particular.
